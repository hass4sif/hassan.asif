A tool for texts, pictures and sounds

Knot

Knot shows what repeats in your material. Repeats are clues to how a thing was made.

How to use it

  1. Choose. Pick Text, Picture or Sound below. Add your own material, or try an example.
  2. Read. Knot shows what repeats. Each result says what Knot found. Tap Read more to see what it does, why, and what it misses.
  3. Undo. Use the repeats as clues. Write down three steps you think made your material.
  4. Redo. Give your three steps to a partner. Can they make your material again from the steps alone?
  5. Check. Send your tile to the room. The tiles join into one pattern on the screen, and the group compares them.

Knot reads your file in this browser. Your file stays on your device. When you press Send, a small tile goes to the room: a few numbers and a name.

    How Knot reads a text, with sources

    Knot splits the text into sentences, or into lines for a poem, a song or a list. It reads each word in small letters. In Arabic script it removes vowel marks, Qur’anic marks and tatweel, and it treats the Arabic and Persian forms of alef, kaf and yeh as one letter, so a word typed two ways counts once. Urdu ے stays apart from ی. English plural endings, and endings in “ing”, count as one word. Knot skips short, common words; tap one to count it.

    To compare with chance, Knot shuffles your own words 199 times and counts again. Then it says if your text sits above, below or among the shuffled copies. This matters: two words fall in reverse order by chance one time in three (Edwards and Edwards).

    How a picture repeats

    Picture

    Choose a photo of a pattern: tiles, cloth, a page, a screen. Knot looks for what repeats: stripes, line directions, stars, and the smallest tile that builds the pattern. Photos of people and places usually show no repeat. That is a result too.

    On a phone you can take the photo here. On a computer, drop or paste a picture. Tap the picture to test for a star at that spot.

    How Knot reads a picture, with sources

    Knot cuts the largest square from the middle of the picture and makes it grey. The stripe map is that square’s Fourier power spectrum, the same kind of picture a crystal makes under X-rays. Each spot stands for one set of stripes: far from the centre means thin stripes, and the angle shows their direction. The map does not show where the stripes sit.

    For the repeating tile, Knot slides a copy of the picture over itself and keeps two slides where it lands (Liu, Collins and Tsin 2004). The line rose counts which way the edges run. For the star test, Knot turns the picture around one point by a half, a third and so on, up to a sixteenth, and checks for mirrors. A score near 1 means a close match.

    What comes back in a recording

    Sound

    Choose a recording or a short video. Knot draws the sound as a picture, follows the pitch of the main voice, finds the pauses, and marks the parts that come back.

    A voice memo, a song, a recitation, or the sound of a video. On a phone you can record a video here. Knot reads the first minute. Tap the sound picture to play from that moment.

    main pitchphrases, between pausesstrongest return

    What Knot found

    Knot measures time, pitch and returns. It does not name a maqām, check tajwīd, identify a reciter or judge a recitation.

    How Knot reads a sound, with sources

    Knot cuts the first minute into short, overlapping slices and measures the energy in each, from 50 to 8,000 vibrations a second. That makes the sound picture, also called a spectrogram. Knot finds the main pitch by subharmonic summation (Hermes 1988) and counts it in cents from the voice’s own home note, the note it comes back to most. A pause is a quiet stretch of a quarter second or more.

    The returns map compares the sound of each moment with each other moment. Parts that repeat draw bright lines next to the diagonal (Foote 1999).

    From your result to the room

    Your tile

    Knot turns your result into a girih tile. The colour shows the kind of material. The line weight shows how much comes back. A filled centre marks a strong repeat, such as a refrain, a repeat unit or a part of a sound that comes back. Tiles meet at the middle of their edges, so the lines run on from your tile into your neighbour’s.

    In a session, the screen at the front shows a room code. Type it here and press Join. Then you can send your tile to the room.

    Knot runs in your browser. Your texts, pictures and recordings stay on your device. A tile leaves when you press Send.
